    Chapter 1

    Why fast charging is hard

    Power is voltage times current. There are only two ways to push more watts into a phone, and both of them cause problems.

    Old chargers delivered 5 volts at 1 amp — five watts. To reach 45 watts you either raise the current or raise the voltage.

    Raising current is expensive. Resistive loss in a cable goes up with the square of current: P_loss = I² × R. Double the current and you quadruple the heat in the cable, the connector pins, and the phone's internal traces. At 9 amps a USB-C cable would need copper thick enough to be uncomfortable to coil.

    Raising voltage is cheap — but a lithium cell only wants about 3.7–4.45 volts. Anything higher has to be stepped back down inside the phone, and every conversion stage loses a percentage as heat, right next to a battery that hates being warm.

    Modern fast charging threads this needle: deliver a moderately high voltage over the cable to keep current low, then convert it efficiently inside the phone — or skip the conversion entirely by matching the charger output to the battery voltage directly. Drag the slider to see the trade-off.

    Chapter 3

    The flyback: storing energy in a magnet

    Almost every charger under about 65 watts uses the same topology. It works in two beats.

    The transformer in a charger is not really a transformer in the textbook sense — it is a pair of coupled inductors used as an energy bucket. Here is the cycle, repeated a few hundred thousand times a second:

    Beat one — switch on. The GaN transistor connects the primary winding across the 300 V bus. Current ramps up through the winding, storing energy in the magnetic field of the ferrite core. The secondary diode is reverse-biased, so nothing comes out yet. Energy goes in, nothing comes out.

    Beat two — switch off. The transistor opens. The collapsing magnetic field reverses the voltage across the windings, the secondary side now conducts, and the stored energy dumps into the output capacitor. Nothing goes in, energy comes out.

    The ratio of on-time to off-time — the duty cycle — decides how much energy moves per cycle, and therefore the output voltage. That is the single knob the controller turns.

    flyback cycle · step through the two beats

    Because energy only crosses the barrier as a magnetic field, there is no conductive path from mains to output. This isolation is the entire reason you can hold a charging phone in the bath-adjacent real world without dying — and the reason counterfeit chargers that skimp on transformer insulation are genuinely lethal.

    Chapter 4

    The control loop: how it knows what to do

    Chopping the bus is easy. Chopping it by exactly the right amount, thousands of times a second, while the load jumps around, is the actual engineering.

    For an ideal flyback, output voltage follows a single equation:

    V_out = V_in × (D / (1 − D)) × (N_s / N_p)

    where D is duty cycle — the fraction of each period the switch is on — and N_s/N_p is the turns ratio. The turns ratio is fixed when the transformer is wound. The bus voltage wanders with the mains. So duty cycle is the only thing left to adjust, and it is adjusted every single cycle.

    Continuous versus discontinuous mode

    If the core fully empties before the next switch-on, the converter is in discontinuous conduction mode. If current is still flowing when the next cycle begins, it is continuous. DCM gives easier control and lets the secondary device turn on at zero current; CCM moves more power through the same core but stresses components with higher peak-to-average currents. Chargers usually sit in DCM at light load and slide toward CCM near full load, and the controller has to stay stable across that transition.

    Valley switching

    After the core empties in DCM, the drain voltage rings. Turning the transistor back on at the bottom of one of those rings — the valley — means switching across the smallest possible voltage, which cuts the energy lost per transition. This is why the switching frequency of a real charger is not a fixed number: it drifts as the controller hunts for valleys under changing load. Hearing a faint chirp from a charger under varying load is often this frequency wandering through the audible band.

    Closing the loop across an isolation barrier

    The primary controller cannot measure the output directly — there is no wire. A shunt reference on the secondary side compares the output against an internal 2.5 V reference and sinks current through an optocoupler LED in proportion to the error. The phototransistor on the primary side reports that error, and the controller adjusts duty cycle to null it.

    That loop has a speed limit. Make it too slow and a sudden load step causes a deep voltage dip before correction arrives. Make it too fast and the loop overshoots, rings, and can oscillate outright. The compensation network — usually one resistor and one capacitor next to the shunt reference — is what sets the balance.

    Chapter 5

    Why GaN made chargers shrink

    The size of a charger is set almost entirely by its transformer, and the size of the transformer is set by how fast you can switch.

    Each switching cycle moves one packet of energy. If you switch twice as often, each packet can be half as big — which means a smaller core, fewer turns of wire, and a smaller capacitor to smooth the result. So designers want the highest switching frequency they can get.

    The limit is loss. Every time a transistor turns on or off it spends a moment in the resistive middle ground, dissipating heat. Silicon MOSFETs take relatively long to make that transition and carry a chunk of gate charge that must be shovelled in and out each cycle. Push a silicon design past roughly 100–150 kHz and switching loss eats the gains.

    Gallium nitride transistors have much lower gate charge and far lower output capacitance, so they cross that resistive middle ground faster. Same power, less loss per transition, which unlocks 300 kHz to over 1 MHz. Slide the frequency to see what it does to the magnetics.

    Chapter 6

    Where the missing watts go

    A 45 W charger pulls about 48 W from the wall. Those three extra watts are not one thing — they are five things that trade against each other.

    Conduction loss is current squared times resistance, in the switch channel, the windings, and the PCB copper. It grows with the square of load, so it dominates at full power and nearly vanishes at light load.

    Switching loss is paid per transition, not per watt. It is roughly constant with load and scales with frequency — which is precisely the tension in chapter 5: higher frequency shrinks the magnetics but multiplies this term. Soft switching techniques like valley switching exist to attack it.

    Core loss comes from hysteresis and eddy currents in the ferrite, growing with frequency and flux swing. Rectification loss is what the synchronous MOSFET still drops. And the controller itself needs a few tens of milliwatts just to exist.

    Notice what happens at light load in the tool below. Because switching loss is load-independent, efficiency collapses at low output unless the controller does something about it — which is why it switches to burst mode, firing a short cluster of pulses then sleeping. That is also how a modern charger meets no-load regulations measured in tens of milliwatts.

    The EMI tax

    Every one of those fast transitions is also a radio transmitter. A 300 kHz square wave with nanosecond edges carries harmonic energy well into the tens of megahertz, and conducted emission limits apply from 150 kHz upward. GaN makes this harder, not easier: faster edges mean more high-frequency content.

    Designers fight back with the common-mode choke, X and Y capacitors, careful loop-area minimisation on the PCB, sometimes a shield winding in the transformer, and occasionally deliberate frequency dithering to spread the spectrum rather than concentrate it in narrow spikes. A charger that passes emissions with 6 dB of margin represents real layout work — and is exactly what a counterfeit deletes.

    Chapter 7

    The negotiation, message by message

    Before a single extra watt flows, the charger and the phone hold a conversation on the CC pin. Run it and watch.

    A USB-C port starts at a safe 5 volts, always. The charger advertises what it can do; the device picks one profile and asks for it; the charger confirms, switches its output, and only then tells the device the new voltage is ready. Nothing is assumed — if either side goes quiet, the link falls back to 5 V.

    The messages are sent using biphase-mark-coded packets on the CC line at about 300 kbit/s, with CRC checks and mandatory acknowledgements. Pick a device and plug it in.

    Chapter 8

    PPS and direct charging

    The most consequential change in fast charging was not more watts. It was letting the phone move the charger's output voltage continuously.

    Under fixed PDO charging the charger sends, say, 9 V. The battery wants 3.9 V. Something inside the phone has to bridge that gap, and that something is a buck converter running at perhaps 89 % efficiency. At 30 W delivered that is over three watts of heat generated inside the phone, millimetres from the cell it is trying to fill.

    PPS changes the arrangement. An Augmented PDO advertises a continuous range — commonly 3.3 to 11 V in 20 mV steps, with current adjustable in 50 mA steps. The phone measures its own cell, calculates the voltage it needs, and asks for exactly that. It must re-request at least every ten seconds or the contract expires and the port drops back to 5 V, which is a deliberate safety interlock.

    The charge pump trick

    Direct charging goes further. Instead of a buck converter, the phone uses a 2:1 switched-capacitor charge pump: capacitors are shuffled between two configurations, halving the input voltage and doubling the current. Because it never stores energy in an inductor and the switches see only half the voltage, it reaches 97–98 % efficiency.

    The catch is that it only divides by exactly two. The charger must therefore supply almost precisely twice the cell voltage — around 7.4 V at 3.7 V per cell, drifting to 8.9 V as the cell fills. Only PPS can track that. Divide-by-four pumps exist for higher-voltage packs, with the same constraint at four times the cell voltage.

    What the CC pin is doing underneath

    Before any of this, the two CC pins settle three questions at once. The sink pulls each CC line down through a 5.1 kΩ resistor to ground (Rd); the source pulls up through a resistor or current source (Rp). Whichever CC line shows the pull-down tells the source which way round the plug is inserted — that is how a reversible connector works with no moving parts.

    The value of the source's pull-up encodes the legacy current limit before any PD messages are exchanged: roughly 0.5 A, 1.5 A, or 3.0 A. A device that speaks no PD at all still gets a safe, meaningful answer. Only then do PD packets begin, biphase-mark-coded onto the same line at about 300 kbit/s.

    Why the cable has a chip in it

    Above 3 A the cable itself must identify as capable. An e-marker chip in the plug answers a Discover_Identity query over the second CC line, declaring rated current, voltage, and speed. No e-marker means the contract is capped at 3 A regardless of what the charger or phone can do — which is why a 45 W charger and a capable phone can still underperform on a cheap cable, and the phone will not tell you why.

    PD 3.1 extends the range further with Extended Power Range: 28, 36 and 48 V fixed profiles up to 240 W, gated behind mandatory e-marked cables and additional voltage-safety checks. Those higher rails never appear on a port until both ends have proven they can handle them.

    Chapter 9

    Run a charge cycle

    Watts are not the whole story. Watch what the battery actually accepts over time, and where the heat goes.

    Lithium cells charge in two phases. In constant current, the charger pushes the maximum safe current and the cell voltage climbs steadily. Once the cell reaches its termination voltage, the charger switches to constant voltage: it holds that voltage fixed and current tapers away as the cell fills. The taper is physics, not conservatism — forcing more current at full charge plates lithium metal onto the anode and permanently damages the cell.

    This is why the marketing number only describes the first half of the curve. Run the simulation at different wattages and watch how little the last 20 % changes.

    Three details are worth staring at. The routed slot across the middle is the isolation barrier — a physical gap enforcing the creepage distance safety standards demand between mains-connected copper and anything a user can reach. Only the transformer and the optocoupler cross it, and both do so without a conductive path.

    Height is set by the bulk capacitor and the transformer. Every generation of shrinkage traces back to making one of those two smaller, which is the whole story of chapter 5.

    Loop area is invisible but decides whether the board passes emissions. The path from bulk capacitor to switch to transformer and back carries the fastest current edges in the design; the physical area that loop encloses is the area of the antenna it accidentally forms. Good layouts keep it small enough to cover with a fingernail. Turn on power flow to trace it.

    Chapter 11

    Efficiency, heat, and what fakes leave out

    Where the missing watts go, and which corners counterfeit chargers cut.

    A good 45 W charger runs at roughly 91–94 % efficiency at full load. The missing 3–4 watts become heat, and they come from four places: conduction loss in the switch and synchronous rectifier, core and winding loss in the transformer, the forward drop of remaining diodes, and the controller's own consumption. Designers trade these against each other constantly — a bigger core cuts magnetic loss but adds cost and volume.

    Standby matters too. Regulations like the EU and US external-power-supply rules cap no-load draw at well under 100 mW, so controllers drop into burst mode when nothing is attached, waking only briefly to keep the bus alive.

    What gets removed in a counterfeit

    • Isolation distance. The routed slot disappears and primary copper runs within a millimetre of secondary. One whisker of solder or one humid day and mains voltage reaches the USB connector.
    • The Y-capacitor and EMI filter. Cheap to omit, and their absence shows up as touchscreen jitter, audible whine, and interference with nearby radios.
    • Transformer tape layers. Genuine transformers use multiple layers of reinforced insulation and a specified margin at the bobbin ends. Fakes use one layer.
    • Output filtering. Undersized capacitors leave ripple on the output that the phone's own regulators then have to absorb, warming the phone.
    • Honest negotiation. Some fakes advertise PD profiles they cannot actually sustain, then sag or shut down under load.

    The practical test is unglamorous: weight and certification. A real 45 W brick has real magnetics and real capacitors in it, and those things have mass. A suspiciously light charger with a printed-on certification mark is usually exactly what it looks like.

    If a charger buzzes audibly under load, gets hot enough to be uncomfortable to hold, or makes a phone's touchscreen behave erratically while plugged in, stop using it. Each of those is a symptom of a specific missing component.
